Konark Sun Temple

This architectural marvel, conceived as a colossal chariot for the Sun God Surya, has mesmerised visitors for over eight centuries.

The temple comprised a sanctum with a lofty sikhara, a jagamohana & a detached nata-mandira in the same axis, besides numerous subsidiary shrines. The sanctum and the nata-mandira have lost their roof.

The entire structure, a UNESCO World Heritage site, is a symphony of sculpture and symbolism, where every inch tells a story of celestial grandeur and earthly passion.

Jagannath Temple

The current main structure was begun by King Anantavarman Chodaganga Deva of the Eastern Ganga dynasty in the 12th century.

Unlike most temples with stone or metal statues, the primary idols are carved from sacred neem wood (Daru Brahma) and are ritually replaced every 12 to 19 years in a secretive ceremony called Nabakalebara.

The main idols are carved from sacred neem wood (Daru) rather than stone or metal, symbolizing the cyclical nature of life.

Millions gather annually to pull massive, newly built wooden chariots carrying the deities through the streets.

Lingaraj Temple (Bhubaneswar)

The main idol represents both Lord Shiva (Hara) and Lord Vishnu (Hari), showing harmony between two major Hindu groups. Devotees offer both bilva leaves (for Shiva) & tulsi leaves.

The large layout includes the main sanctum (Garbhagriha), a hall for offerings (Bhoga Mandap), a hall for dancing (Natya Shala), and a hall for prayers (Yajna Shala).

The major festivals celebrated at the historic Lingaraj Temple in Bhubaneswar are Maha Shivaratri, Rukuna Rath Yatra, & Chandan Yatra.

Rajarani Temple

Rajarani Temple stands apart from traditional Hindu temples by being devoid of deity icons, classifying itself as a Saivite temple.

Rajarani Temple, recognized by those who matter Declared a National Monument by Archaeological Survey of India.

Additionally, its architectural significance is recognized in books like "The Architecture of India: From Indus Valley to Taj Mahal" and "Art of India: Highlights from Collections at National Museum New Delhi."

Every year between January & February, the grounds host the famous Rajarani Music Festival, celebrating classical Indian music.

Chausath Yogini Temple

The daily prayer and rituals in the temple includes the worship of Bhumandala that is the five elements of nature - water, sky, fire, earth and ether.

The shrine is locally known as Chausathi Yogini, meaning 64 Yoginis (incarnations of Goddess Durga).

Every year in the month of December, the Chausathi Yogini Mahotsav is organized near the temple. Nrutya Prativa, a city-based cultural organization, with support from the Odisha Tourism Department conducts this three-day event, in which classical dance and vocal artists from across the country perform and mesmerize the audience.

Mukteswara Temple

The Mukteswara Temple is a 10th-century Hindu shrine dedicated to Lord Shiva in Old Town, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, widely celebrated as the "Gem of Odisha architecture".

Features a 35-foot-tall sanctuary (vimana) with detailed Nataraja and Kirthimukha carvings.

Leaning Temple of Huma

The Leaning Temple of Huma in Odisha (23 km from Sambalpur) is famous for being one of the only leaning temples in the world. Dedicated to Lord Bimaleshwar (Shiva).

The temple sits on the rocky banks of the Mahanadi River, which is home to abundant schools of sacred Kudo (reddish) fish.

Folklore suggests the Shiva Lingam was discovered by a pious milkman whose cow miraculously fed a particular rock with its milk. Another local legend tells of a woman who was turned into stone while cutting a fish, a statue of which is said to rest near the complex.

Brahmeswara Temple

The Brahmeswara Temple in Odisha, is a Hindu temple built around 1058 CE by Queen Kolavati Devi of the Somavamsi dynasty.

It was built with four Natyasalas at a place known as Siddhatirtha in Ekamra (modern-day Bhubaneswar).

One of the lost inscriptions stated that a Queen Kolavati presented 'many beautiful women' to the temple, and it has been suggested that this is evidence of the 'Devadasi' tradition, which assumed such importance in later Odia temple architecture and temple life.
